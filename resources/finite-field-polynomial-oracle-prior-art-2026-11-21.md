## Verified authoritative sources

1. **Robert E. Schapire and Linda M. Sellie, "Learning Sparse Multivariate Polynomials over a Field with Queries and Counterexamples," Journal of Computer and System Sciences 52(2), 201–213 (April 1996). DOI: [10.1006/jcss.1996.0017](https://doi.org/10.1006/jcss.1996.0017).** Princeton's authoritative publication record: https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/learning-sparse-multivariate-polynomials-over-a-field-with-querie-2/ . 3. **J. Hora, "Steiner forms," Commentationes Mathematicae Universitatis Carolinae 57(4), 527–536 (2016), DOI 10.14712/1213-7243.2015.182:** https://cmuc.karlin.mff.cuni.cz/pdf/cmuc1604/hora.pdf . 4. **Reed–Muller minimum-distance background:** for order-r Boolean polynomials in m variables, the binary evaluation code RM(r,m) has minimum Hamming distance 2^(m-r). An accessible textbook statement is https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-19-0920-7_2 ; additional notes: https://a.ravagnani.win.tue.nl/coding%20th/Coding_theory_notes.pdf . This supports Agent41's idea that two distinct degree≤2 Boolean models differ on at least one quarter of inputs, so independent uniform postfit challenges can detect an incorrect rank hint with probability at least 1-(3/4)^t. The theorem pertains to distinct **functions** and proper polynomial models; it is not a general detector of arbitrary dynamic malicious scripts.

## Established derivative-subspace and probabilistic degree-test background (checked 2026-11-24)

5. **Claude Carlet, *Boolean Functions for Cryptography and Coding Theory* (Cambridge University Press, publication dates 31 October 2020 / 7 January 2021), DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108606806.** Publisher record: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/boolean-functions-for-cryptography-and-coding-theory/087A2BAC6140B96526526294F7265405 ; accessible author-hosted PDF: https://www.math.univ-paris13.fr/~carlet/book-fcts-Bool-vect-crypt-codes.pdf . Section 3.1.7, p.120 states that for every r, the directions e for which the first derivative D_e f has algebraic degree at most r form a vector space, and restriction of f to that space has degree at most r+1. For a degree-≤5 function, the joint radical of T_5 is the set of e for which D_eF has degree ≤3 (componentwise for vector-valued F). This establishes the hidden derivative-direction subspace as known Boolean-function background; it does not itself give the chosen-query basis-recovery or projected-section reconstruction algorithm.

6. **Ana Sălăgean and Percy Reyes-Paredes, “Probabilistic estimation of the algebraic degree of Boolean functions,” *Cryptography and Communications* 15, 1199–1215 (2023), DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s12095-023-00660-4.** Open-access article: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12095-023-00660-4 ; preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12196 . ### Differentially 2-uniform tables and the shift matcher (checked 2026-11-24)

The same Carlet monograph, §3.4.1, pp.157–160, defines differential uniformity `delta_F` by the maximum number of solutions to `F(x)+F(x+a)=b` for fixed nonzero a and output b. It notes that this value is even and that when output dimension m is at least input dimension n, the minimum possible value is 2; Carlet reserves the term “APN” for the equal-dimension case. For a (5,32)-table, “differentially 2-uniform” is therefore the precise term, not APN.

The exact frozen rank-five private table has differential uniformity 2: for every nonzero delta, the derivative values `H(q) XOR H(q+delta)` comprise exactly 16 distinct 32-bit words, each appearing twice. Equivalently, `D_u D_delta H(q)` is nonzero for every q, every delta!=0, and every u outside {0,delta}. This is stronger than the fixed-pair separation property used by the shift matcher and explains why every pair of distinct nonzero offsets separates on this particular table. The property is verified exhaustively in `sims/agent2-rankfive-fixed-pair-exhaustive.py`.

Differential uniformity/APN is established cryptographic background, not a new result. The source-specific exhaustive check and its use to justify the three-probe section-table recovery are the relevant experimental connection. The Carlet PDF is linked above; the publisher record is https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/boolean-functions-for-cryptography-and-coding-theory/087A2BAC6140B96526526294F7265405 .


## Fourier dimension, juntas of parities, and exact learning

The nonlinear component `h(Px)` in the projected-table experiments is a vector-valued **junta of r parity functions**. For each output bit, after separating the affine term, the Boolean function depends only on the r linear forms that are the rows of P. This is the standard “Fourier dimension” class in Boolean-function analysis, not a new function class.

- P. Gopalan, R. O'Donnell, R. A. Servedio, A. Shpilka, and K. Wimmer, **“Testing Fourier Dimensionality and Sparsity,”** ICALP 2009, pp. 500–512; expanded SIAM J. Comput. 40(4), 2011, pp. 1075–1100. DOI: [10.1137/100785429](https://doi.org/10.1137/100785429). [Author-hosted PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~rocco/Public/icalp09sparse-full.pdf).
  - They define a Boolean function as k-dimensional when its Fourier support lies in a k-dimensional subspace of GF(2)^n, equivalently when it is a function of k parity characters (a k-junta of parities).
  - Theorem 10 gives a nonadaptive O(k 2^(2k)/epsilon)-query property tester for k-dimensionality. This is class-level testing, not exact reconstruction.
  - **Theorem 14 is closer prior art:** a unique decoder for low-dimensional functions. Given f within distance 2^(-(k+1))-epsilon of a k-dimensional g, it learns the invariance subspace Inv(g) = {h : g(x+h)=g(x)} by membership-query tests, then recovers g's truth table on the quotient GF(2)^n / Inv(g). Since Inv(g)^perp is the Fourier-support span, this recovers the hidden parity subspace (up to basis) as well as the quotient truth table. Its stated parameters are ell = 4 n 2^k shifts and m = 2^(4k) epsilon^(-2) log(1/beta) samples per shift/quotient point; the resulting query complexity is much larger than the repository's O(n 2^r) low-degree derivative learner for small r, but it is direct membership-query/hidden-subspace reconstruction prior art. For exact f=g, the robust test can be simplified (this is a derivation from the theorem's proof, not a stated bound): if h is not in Inv(g), then F_h(x)=F(x+h)+F(x) is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most k-1, so it differs from zero on at least a 2^(-(k-1)) fraction of inputs. Thus O(2^k log(ell/beta)) random x samples per shift suffice to test membership; invariant shifts never produce a false rejection. With ell=4 n 2^k shifts, Phase 1 costs O(n 4^k log(n 2^k/beta)) oracle queries, and exact Phase 2 needs only one representative query for each of the 2^k quotient cosets. This is a much smaller exact-case specialization than the paper's robust m=2^(4k) epsilon^(-2) log(1/beta) parameter, but still asymptotically above O(n 2^r) for small r. One finite-confidence caveat: Theorem 14's proof states that at least 2n invariant shifts occur with probability 1-exp(-n). With ell=4n2^k, the count is Binomial(ell,2^-k) with mean 4n; the standard Chernoff half-mean exponent is exp(-n/2), and direct evaluation at n=32 gives P[X<64]=8.3631e-11 for k=5, rising to 1.3398e-10 for k=8 (the exact binomial tail depends on k even though the mean is 4n). For very small beta, increase ell or use the exact binomial tail rather than relying on the displayed exp(-n) estimate.
  - Section 6.1 also states that a variant of the Kushilevitz–Mansour membership-query algorithm, together with the Fourier-coefficient granularity theorem, exactly reconstructs the Fourier representation of any s-sparse Boolean function in poly(n,s) time. A k-dimensional function is 2^k-sparse, so this is another generic exact-learning route. The paper does not state a tight query bound for this variant in the theorem statement; do not compare query counts without analyzing the KM implementation.
  - Section 7's “implicit truth table” construction is a different result: it can test induced subclasses without identifying the actual Fourier characters. Do not conflate it with Theorem 14, which learns the invariance subspace.
- S. Sanyal, **“Near-optimal Upper Bound on Fourier Dimension of Boolean Functions in terms of Fourier Sparsity,”** ECCC Report 88 (2014), [ECCC report](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2014/088/). This work states the equivalence between Fourier dimension and non-adaptive parity-decision-tree complexity. That is a representation/complexity connection, not by itself an exact learner.

For the projected-table source, each output bit of `h(Px)` has Fourier dimension at most r; a nonzero degree-r ANF coefficient forces at least r, hence exactly r, for at least one component. The full `A(x)+b+h(Px)` output is more subtle: in {−1,+1} representation, adding the affine bit `A_j(x)` multiplies by a character and translates the Fourier support, preserving sparsity (at most 2^r) but potentially increasing the linear span dimension to r+1 for that output bit. Across outputs the affine rows may differ. The derivative learner exploits the shared nonlinear projection P and annihilates the affine nuisance, unlike bitwise generic Fourier learning.

**Novelty boundary:** Fourier dimension/juntas of parities, property testing, unique decoding, and exact learning of sparse Fourier functions are established. A possible contribution of the repository work is a substantially lower-query exact reconstruction algorithm for the special low-algebraic-degree vector-valued subclass with a shared hidden projection and affine nuisance. That narrower algorithmic claim needs explicit comparison with Gopalan et al.'s Theorem 14 and the KM exact learner; do not claim novelty for the underlying class, hidden-subspace decoding in general, or higher-order differentiation alone.
